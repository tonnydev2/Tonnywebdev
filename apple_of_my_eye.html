An Apple of My Eye

— a story of the girl who never was

It happened on a drizzly Tuesday in the school canteen, the kind of grey afternoon that made the steamed-up windows look like frosted glass. I had just grabbed a cheap tray with a sandwich I didn’t want, scanning for an empty seat among the clatter of trays and half-hearted laughs. That’s when I saw her.

She sat near the back wall, beside the wilting potted fern that nobody ever watered. Her hair the colour of autumn chestnuts tumbled over one shoulder, and when she lifted her head from a worn-out copy of Pablo Neruda’s poetry, I swear the fluorescent lights above flickered into something softer — like candlelight. She had those eyes, wide and honey-warm, that seemed to look right through the grey plastic chairs and into the quiet corners of your heart.

Everything about her was perfect. Not the hollow perfection of filters and magazines, but the real kind — the kind that makes a person feel like they walked out of a forgotten memory. The slight gap between her front teeth that appeared when she smiled at her own thoughts. The tiny scar above her left eyebrow, shaped like a crescent moon. She wore an old college sweatshirt two sizes too big, sleeves rolled up to her elbows, revealing thin silver bracelets that jingled every time she turned a page.

I stood frozen, tray wobbling in my hand, as she looked up and caught me staring. Instead of turning away in disgust, she simply tilted her head and — smiled. “You look lost,” she said, voice like the first sip of honeyed tea on a sore throat. I muttered something stupid about the queue for cutlery. She laughed, a quiet, breathy sound, and gestured to the empty chair across from her. “Sit. You look like you need poetry more than a fork.”

We talked for what felt like hours, but the canteen clock barely moved. She told me her name was Elara — the same as a moon of Jupiter, she said, because her father was an astronomer who loved impossible things. She was majoring in linguistics and minoring in classical guitar. She hated mint chocolate but loved the smell of rain on hot asphalt. When she laughed, two tiny dimples appeared like secret signatures only for me. I found myself telling her things I never told my closest friends — how I was afraid of growing up into someone ordinary, how I still had nightmares about my dog that ran away when I was nine. She didn't flinch. She just reached across the table and placed her warm palm over my knuckles. “Ordinary is just a word scared people invented,” she whispered. “You’re a symphony, not a bell.”

And the best part? She looked at me like I was the strange miracle, not her. Her fingers traced the rim of her apple juice carton as she asked about my dreams, my favourite song, the name of my first stuffed toy. No one had ever done that before. No one.

The next days blurred into a soft golden haze. We met at the same canteen table every afternoon. She started bringing me oranges because I once mentioned I liked the smell of citrus. I started learning three words of her favourite dead language every night just to see the surprise in her eyes. She had a laugh that could dismantle any bad day. I was completely, helplessly, wonderfully in love. One afternoon, as the autumn sun dripped through the blinds and painted her in stripes of amber, she turned to me with that crooked smile and said, “You know, you’re the first person who ever made me feel like the storm isn’t something to survive, but something to dance in.”

I opened my mouth to tell her that she was the apple of my eye — the brightest, most perfect fruit on the whole tree of my ordinary life — but just as the words formed on my tongue…

“Dude! WAKE UP! You’re gonna be late for calc – and you’re smiling like an idiot in your sleep!”

A violent shake. The smell of burnt coffee and stale socks. My roommate Leo’s stubbly face two inches from mine. I blinked. The canteen was gone. Elara was gone. The honey-warm eyes, the dimples, the Neruda book, the silver bracelets — all devoured by the grey morning light leaking through our torn dorm blinds. My pillow was damp. My chest felt like someone had carved a hole right where she used to sit across from me.

“You okay, man?” Leo frowned, scratching his messy hair. “You were whispering some name… Elara? Did you finally talk to that girl from psych class?” I shook my head slowly, throat tight. “No,” I whispered. “No, I didn’t.” Leo shrugged, tossed me a wrinkled t-shirt and left for the bathroom. I lay there, staring at the water stain on the ceiling, replaying every detail of a girl who never existed. The apple of my eye. The most perfect someone I would never meet.

— some storms we dance in only while we're sleeping.
